Integrating digital methods into Arabic philology

The development of digital resources and methods has outpaced their integration into research practice.

  • The evidence: thousands of Arabic works spanning fourteen centuries are now available for computational analysis.
  • The prevailing practice: research remains concentrated on individual texts, limited selections, and narrowly defined problems.
  • The challenge: build a cumulative understanding of social history, cultural production, the development of textual types (“genres”), and the historical development of the Arabic language itself.

What becomes possible when we study the Arabic written tradition as an interconnected whole?

Part 1 · State of the field

Digital resources and methods have developed faster than their integration into research practice. Thousands of Arabic works spanning fourteen centuries are now available for computational analysis. Yet research remains concentrated on individual texts, limited selections, and narrowly defined problems. The challenge is to build a cumulative understanding of social history, cultural production, the development of textual types, or “genres,” and the historical development of the Arabic language itself. So the question for this lecture: what becomes possible when we study the Arabic written tradition as an interconnected whole?

Part 1 · State of the field

Four years later, Brill published Cornelis van Lit's Among Digitized Manuscripts in the Handbook of Oriental Studies. It discusses how digitized manuscripts differ from physical ones, ranks twenty online repositories by criteria such as resolution, color, and download options, and introduces TEI, IIIF, cataloging, and image processing with Python and OpenCV, ending with an autobiographical postscript. Reviewers were kind: Philip Bockholt called it a welcome contribution (MIDÉO 37, 2022). My own reading is less generous. The book explains formats and tools, but it does not pose a historical or philological question and it produces no analytical result. That is a problem for the field: it presents digital humanities as a technical service rather than a way of doing research. [Bockholt, review of van Lit, MIDÉO 37 (2022): 215–218, journals.openedition.org/mideo/8299. What should digital humanities in our field be, and what can it give us?

Part 1 · State of the field

My own book, published by Brill this year in the same series as van Lit's, is an attempt to propose a coherent vision of what digital humanities can be for our field. It makes the case in three steps. Why: the written tradition is too large to be understood text by text, and we need ways to read it as a whole. How: a corpus designed for research — OpenITI, its mARkdown annotation, URIs, and versioned releases, the foundation that KITAB and EIS1600 share. And what it gives: three case studies with historical results — the history of a term (the six books), a typology of Arabic writing, and a periodization based on language and themes. This is the segue: what should digital humanities in our field be, and what can a proper implementation give us? The Vision

The work I’ll show you starts from one proposition:

Case 2. Modeling Textual Typology

  1. [B] Biographical type (tarājim): mostly collections of biographies
  2. [E] Exegetical type: interpretations of the Qurʾān (tafsīr)
  3. [G] Graeco-Arabic (“Greek-esque”) type (yūnāniyyāt): translations from Greek and works on the development of Greek philosophy in Arabic (falsafat, predominantly Aristotelian)
  4. [H] Historical type (tawārīḫ): mainly chronicles
  5. [F] Legal type (fiqh): a broad variety of legal texts
  6. [L] Linguistic type: a broad variety of grammatical, rhetorical, and lexicographical texts (balāġat, naḥw, maʿājim luġawiyyat)
  7. [M] Medical type (ṭibb): mainly texts on Galenic medicine
  8. [P] Poetic type (šiʿr): mainly collections of poetry (dīwāns)
  9. [K] “Scholastic-theological” type: works of kalām
  10. [Ḥ] Tradition-based type: collections of ḥadīṯ
Part 2 · My contributions

Textual types over 14 centuries

  • The [F] legal type (represented by approximately 1,600 titles)
    • steady growth in prevalence over time, peaking around 1000–1200/1592–1786. It is the dominant type in the corpus overall.
  • The [Ḥ] Tradition-based type (approximately 1,250 titles)
    • pronounced early dominance, peaking around 400/1010, and then declines sharply.
  • The [B] biographical type (approximately 1,050 titles)
    • grows steadily until the end of the Mamlūk period, after which it declines.
  • The [L] linguistic type (approximately 790 titles)
    • somewhat more prevalent in the early period but generally remains steady across the centuries.
  • The [E] exegetical type (approximately 750 titles)
    • grows gradually, its numbers remaining within a fairly consistent range over time.
Part 2 · My contributions

Each type has a distinct trajectory. The counts are approximate: a text is assigned to a type at a 51% match threshold, numbers are rounded, and the periods before 100/719 and after 1400/1980 are excluded. Legal writing grows steadily and dominates overall; tradition-based writing dominates early and becomes marginal after 800/1398; biographical writing grows until the end of the Mamlūk period; linguistic writing stays fairly steady; and exegesis surges in the Mamlūk period. The Arabic written tradition as an interconnected whole

For centuries, scholars writing in Arabic organized knowledge into branches such as law, medicine, history, and Qurʾānic interpretation. We have rich studies of individual branches and extensive bibliographies. What remains unclear is how these branches fitted together: how distinct they were, how they interacted, and how those relationships changed across time and space. ArabesQuery addresses this question through systematic analysis of the texts, in dialogue with the tradition’s own classifications.

To conclude

My proposition is that:

digital Arabic philology allows us to study the Arabic written tradition as a whole through a unified, machine-readable corpus. We can ask how scholarly communities changed, how texts drew on one another, how their typology developed, and how Arabic itself evolved. And we can move from a single passage to patterns across centuries and regions, then return to the passages and their contexts to see what those patterns mean. And never lose the sight of the big picture.
